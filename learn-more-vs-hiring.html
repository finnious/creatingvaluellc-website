The hire feels safe. The math rarely is.

Hiring a technical person feels like the responsible move — a real employee, on the team, owning the problem. But look at what the hire actually asks of you:

  • Months before value. Sourcing, interviewing, offer, notice period, onboarding, ramp. You're often two quarters in before the first useful thing ships.
  • A permanent cost for a project-shaped need. Most of what you need is a burst of building — get the systems connected, get the reports flowing — not a full-time seat forever. A salary doesn't flex down when the build is done.
  • A single skill ceiling. One person is strong in one area. The work you need spans data, automation, reporting, and judgment about what to build. Few individuals cover all of it, and the ones who do don't take a mid-market salary.
  • Management overhead. Now you're reviewing, unblocking, and retaining them — on top of running the business.

What "build instead" actually looks like

We take the technical-decision-maker role on a project basis. We decide what gets built, build it, and hand it back running on your own systems. You get the judgment of a technology leader and the output of a builder — without the salary, the ramp, or the department to manage.

The method: AI pair-programming lets one senior operator move like a small team. In one engagement, that meant 15 design iterations in a single 2-hour session and a full inventory pipeline going from discovered-in-audit to deployed-and-running in one working day. No tickets. No handoffs. No meetings about meetings.
